Updated Drawback Review Report: Doxxing Framework and Civil Redress

This report provides a technically precise analysis of the doxxing framework and civil compensation mechanisms under the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (PDPO). It is designed to resolve compliance ambiguities regarding the two-tier offence structure, the statutory thresholds of harm, and the availability of non-monetary civil redress.

1. Clarifying the Two-Tier Doxxing Offence Structure

The doxxing framework distinguishes between summary and indictable offences based on the absence of the data subject's "relevant consent" and whether the disclosure results in actual harm. "Relevant consent" includes the consent of the data subject or a "relevant person" (e.g., a parent or guardian) as defined in s.2(1).

* s.64(3A) Summary Offence: This first-tier offence focuses on the behavior and intent of the discloser. It is triggered when a person discloses personal data without the relevant consent of the data subject with the intent to cause specified harm, or being reckless as to whether specified harm would be (or would likely be) caused. Liability is established regardless of whether harm actually occurs. Penalties are governed by s.64(3B) (Level 6 fine and 2 years imprisonment).
* s.64(3C) Indictable Offence: This second-tier offence is triggered when the elements of s.64(3A) are met and the disclosure actually causes specified harm to the data subject or any family member. Due to the realized victimization, it carries the higher penalties specified in s.64(3D) ($1M fine and 5 years imprisonment).

Outcome-Based Distinction

* s.64(3A) Summary Offence: Triggered by intent or recklessness; no proof of actual harm is required for conviction.
* s.64(3C) Indictable Offence: Triggered only when the disclosure results in actual harm to the data subject or a family member.

2. Statutory Definition of 'Specified Harm' under s.64(6)

To ensure precise application of doxxing provisions, s.64(6) provides an exhaustive definition of "specified harm." For a charge under s.64(3C) to be sustained, or for the intent/recklessness in s.64(3A) to be proven, the harm must fall into one of the following four categories:

* Harassment and Intimidation: Harassment, molestation, pestering, threat, or intimidation to the person.
* Bodily or Psychological Harm: Bodily harm or psychological harm to the person.
* Safety Concerns: Harm causing the person reasonably to be concerned for their personal safety or well-being.
* Property Damage: Damage to the property of the person.

Scope of Protection: The doxxing provisions extend beyond the primary data subject. Under s.64(6), "specified harm" includes the aforementioned categories when caused to any family member of the data subject.

3. Civil Compensation and 'Injury to Feelings' under s.66

Beyond criminal prosecution, the Ordinance provides a mechanism for individuals to seek personal redress through civil action.

* Right to Compensation (s.66): A data subject who suffers damage due to a data user's contravention of any requirement under the Ordinance is entitled to seek compensation.
* Non-Monetary Damage (s.66(2)): The definition of "damage" for which a data subject is entitled to compensation specifically includes injury to feelings.

Technical Note: This provision allows for civil action even when direct financial loss cannot be quantified. In such cases, the court may award compensation for emotional distress or psychological impact resulting from the privacy violation. Crucially, this remains a civil remedy led by the plaintiff (on a balance of probabilities), which is distinct from the government-led criminal penalties under s.64.

5. Compliance Safeguards: Defences and Vicarious Liability

The Ordinance provides specific limitations on liability to protect those acting lawfully or those who have implemented robust compliance programs.

Mitigation Factors

1. Statutory Defences for Doxxing (s.64(4)): It is a defence to a doxxing charge if the person reasonably believed:
  * The disclosure was necessary for preventing or detecting crime.
  * The disclosure was required or authorized by law or a court order.
  * The disclosure was made with the "relevant consent" of the data subject or the data user (depending on the specific charge).
  * The disclosure was made solely for a lawful news activity AND the person had reasonable grounds to believe that publishing or broadcasting was in the public interest.
2. Employer's Defence for Vicarious Liability (s.65(3)): An employer is not civilly liable for an employee's contravention if they can prove (on a balance of probabilities) that they had taken all practicable steps to prevent the act. This is a proactive requirement; employers must implement training, internal policies, and monitoring mechanisms before a contravention occurs to successfully invoke this safeguard.
3. Standard of Proof: In criminal doxxing prosecutions, the government must prove the case beyond reasonable doubt. In civil proceedings for compensation, the plaintiff must prove the contravention on a balance of probabilities.
